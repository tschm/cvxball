\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We record two active-set methods for the smallest enclosing ball problem, one on
each side of it, and the invariance properties an implementation of either must
preserve. The first ascends the dual --- a quadratic program over the unit
simplex --- and holds no enclosing ball until it terminates; the second, of
Fischer, G\"artner and Kutz, deflates an enclosing ball and is feasible
throughout. Both terminate at an exact basis rather than at an interior-point
tolerance, both cost one solve or projection of order at most $d$ per iteration
beside one sweep of the cloud, and both repair the same factorisation of the same
support. What separates them is what they can hand back: dual weights that
certify optimality in one pass, against a ball that is already valid at every
step. On Gaussian clouds to $d=1.6\cdot10^4$ they agree on the support set and
differ by a small constant factor in time.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Sylvester stated the problem in 1857 in a single sentence~\cite{sylvester1857}:
given a finite set of points in the plane, find the smallest circle containing
all of them. It has been a standard example ever since --- short enough to state
in a line, geometric enough to draw, and just hard enough that the obvious
algorithms are wrong. It is the $1$-centre facility location problem, where one
facility is placed so that the farthest customer travels as little as possible.
It is the primitive under bounding-sphere hierarchies in graphics and collision
detection. As the minimum enclosing ball of a set of feature vectors it is the
core-set problem behind kernel machines. And it is a fixture of solver
documentation: Hexaly ships it as a modelling template, in which the centre's
coordinates are the decision variables and the radius is an intermediate
expression built from \texttt{sqrt}, \texttt{pow} and \texttt{max}, minimised
directly~\cite{hexaly}; CGAL ships it as \texttt{Min\_sphere\_d}, which is
Welzl's recursion with the move-to-front heuristic for small inputs and
G\"artner's method for large ones.

The algorithmic history is largely a computational-geometry one, and largely the
work of two people. Welzl's randomised incremental method~\cite{welzl1991} solves
it in expected $O(n)$ time for fixed $d$, recursing over subsets with a few points
forced onto the boundary; the constant grows superexponentially in $d$, and much
of what followed is a response to that. G\"artner's robust
implementation~\cite{gaertner1999} addressed the fragility of the recursion's
predicates in floating point, and is the other half of what CGAL runs.
G\"artner and
Sch\"onherr~\cite{gaertner2000} recast the problem as a quadratic program and
solved it exactly, reserving judgment past a few hundred dimensions. Fischer,
G\"artner and Kutz~\cite{fischer2003} then left the recursion behind altogether
for a pivoting method that deflates an enclosing ball, carrying a dynamic $QR$ of
the support's edge matrix and reporting $d$ in the thousands. Elzinga and
Hearn~\cite{elzinga1972} had given a finite algorithm of that second kind two
decades before Welzl.

What returned me to the problem was a pair of recent experiments with active-set
methods elsewhere: the Goldfarb--Idnani dual method for strictly convex quadratic
programs, whose invariants hold under bound constraints and quietly stop holding
under general ones~\cite{schmelzer2026gi}, and a conjugate-gradient solver wrapped
in a primal--dual active-set loop for non-negativity
constraints~\cite{schmelzer2026nncg}. The smallest enclosing ball is that same
machinery on the simplest feasible set there is. Its dual is a concave quadratic
over the unit simplex; the active set is the set of points on the boundary
sphere; and the certificate is geometric rather than algebraic --- the points
carrying weight are exactly the points at distance $R$, which a caller can check
in one pass.

What follows records two methods on that structure, at equal length and with
equal claim: the dual ascent of Section~\ref{sec:method}, and the primal
deflation of Section~\ref{sec:fgk}, which is the pivoting method of Fischer,
G\"artner and Kutz~\cite{fischer2003}. They keep the same support set, solve the same subproblem
and repair the same factorisation (Section~\ref{sec:qr}); they need the same care
with tolerances (Section~\ref{sec:invariance}); and on every input measured in
Section~\ref{sec:numerics} they agree on the ball, on its support, and to within a
small constant factor on time. Where they part is in what they hold while they
run, and so in what they can promise if interrupted.

\section{The problem and its two sides}
\label{sec:dual}

For $S=\{p_1,\dots,p_n\}\subset\R^d$ the smallest enclosing ball is the solution of
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:primal}
  \min_{x,r}\ r \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \norm{p_i-x}\le r,\ i=1,\dots,n,
\end{equation}
a second-order cone program in $(r,x)\in\R^{1+d}$ with $n$ blocks
$(r,\,p_i-x)\in\mathcal{Q}^{d+1}$. Existence follows from coercivity of
$f(x)=\max_i\norm{p_i-x}$ and uniqueness from strict convexity of $f^2$ along any
segment; we write $(R,x^\star)$ for the optimum.

Let $\Delta_n=\{u\in\R^n: u\ge0,\ \mathbf{1}\T u=1\}$, and for $u\in\Delta_n$ put
$x(u)=\sum_i u_ip_i$ and
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:phi}
  \varphi(u)=\sum_i u_i\norm{p_i}^2-\norm{x(u)}^2 ,
\end{equation}
a concave quadratic. Everything below rests on one identity, valid for all
$y\in\R^d$ and $u\in\Delta_n$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:id}
  \sum_i u_i\norm{p_i-y}^2
    \;=\; \sum_i u_i\norm{p_i}^2-2\,x(u)\T y+\norm{y}^2
    \;=\; \varphi(u)+\norm{x(u)-y}^2 .
\end{equation}
Both steps are mechanical. The first expands the square and collects terms,
using $\sum_iu_ip_i=x(u)$ on the cross term and $\mathbf{1}\T u=1$ on
$\norm{y}^2$; the second adds and subtracts $\norm{x(u)}^2$, which
by~\eqref{eq:phi} leaves $\varphi(u)$ beside a completed square. What makes it
an equality rather than a bound is that no term has been discarded: the weights
enter only through $x(u)$ and through $\sum_iu_i\norm{p_i}^2$, and $\varphi$ is
defined as exactly the difference of those two. So this is the parallel-axis
identity of mechanics, or the bias--variance decomposition: measuring from
anywhere other than the weighted mean costs precisely $\norm{x(u)-y}^2$,
whatever the weights, and $\varphi(u)$ is what is left when $y$ is the mean.

\begin{proposition}
  \label{prop:duality}
  $R^2=\max_{u\in\Delta_n}\varphi(u)$, and $x^\star=x(w)$ for any maximiser $w$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  If $B(y,r)\supseteq S$ then the left side of~\eqref{eq:id} is at most $r^2$, so
  $\varphi(u)\le r^2$ for every $u$; taking the optimum gives
  $\max\varphi\le R^2$. Conversely let $T=\{p_i:\norm{p_i-x^\star}=R\}$. If
  $x^\star\notin\conv T$, separate: there is $v$ with $v\T(p-x^\star)>0$ on $T$,
  and $\norm{p-(x^\star+tv)}^2=R^2-2tv\T(p-x^\star)+t^2\norm{v}^2<R^2$ for small
  $t>0$, while the finitely many points off $T$ stay strictly inside --- so the
  radius decreases, a contradiction. Hence $x^\star=\sum_i w_ip_i$ for some
  $w\in\Delta_n$ supported on $T$, and $y=x^\star$ in~\eqref{eq:id} gives
  $\varphi(w)=\sum_iw_i\norm{p_i-x^\star}^2=R^2$. The same substitution forces
  $\norm{x(w)-x^\star}^2\le0$ for any maximiser.
\end{proof}

Since $\partial\varphi/\partial u_i=\norm{p_i-x(u)}^2-\norm{x(u)}^2$, the
first-order conditions for $\max_{\Delta_n}\varphi$ read, after cancelling the
common term,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:kkt}
  \norm{p_i-x}=R\ \ (w_i>0),\qquad \norm{p_i-x}\le R\ \ (w_i=0),
\end{equation}
the geometric certificate: the points carrying weight lie on the boundary sphere
and the rest are enclosed. By Carath\'eodory the support may be taken of size at
most $d+1$, so~\eqref{eq:primal} is determined by at most $d+1$ of the $n$ points
and the problem is combinatorial in the choice of that subset.

Read forwards,~\eqref{eq:kkt} is a search for weights: keep a set of points on the
sphere, adjust the weights on it, and stop when no point lies outside. Read
backwards it is a search for balls: keep a ball that already contains $S$ with a
set $T$ on its boundary, and shrink it until its centre falls inside $\conv T$.
The second reading rests on a fact due to Seidel, recorded
in~\cite{gaertner2000} and taken as Lemma~1 by Fischer, G\"artner and
Kutz~\cite{fischer2003}: if $T$ lies on the boundary of a ball $B$ with centre
$c$, then $B$ is the smallest ball enclosing $T$ if and only if $c\in\conv T$.
That is a stopping certificate for $S$ as well. If in addition $S\subseteq B$
then $B\supseteq\mathrm{seb}(S)$, while $T\subseteq S$ gives
$\mathrm{seb}(T)\subseteq\mathrm{seb}(S)$; with $B=\mathrm{seb}(T)$ the two
inclusions close, and $B=\mathrm{seb}(S)$ by uniqueness. So a ball containing the
cloud, carrying $T$ on its boundary, and centred inside $\conv T$ is \emph{the}
answer --- and $\conv T$ is the only thing left to check. Sections~\ref{sec:method} and~\ref{sec:fgk} are those two readings made into
methods --- the second being theirs. They keep the same
kind of state --- a subset of $S$ that will end up on the boundary, and a point in
its affine hull --- and differ in which feasibility they refuse to give up. The
first stays dual feasible, so by Proposition~\ref{prop:duality} its radius rises
to $R$ from below and its ball encloses nothing until the last iteration; the
second stays primal feasible, so its radius falls to $R$ from above and every
iterate is an answer, merely not yet the best one.

\section{The dual active-set method}
\label{sec:method}

Maintain a working set $F$ and weights $u>0$ on it supported there and summing to
one:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:as-invariant}
  u\in\Delta_n,\qquad \mathrm{supp}(u)\subseteq F,\qquad
  \varphi(u)\ \text{non-decreasing},
\end{equation}
the dual counterpart of the invariant~\eqref{eq:fgk-invariant} carried by the
method of Section~\ref{sec:fgk}. This is a primal active-set method run on the
dual~\eqref{eq:phi}: the free variables are the weights of the support, every
subproblem solves one face exactly, and feasibility for the original problem
arrives only at the end --- by Proposition~\ref{prop:duality},
$\sqrt{\varphi(u)}$ is a lower bound on $R$ at every iterate, so the ball on hand
is too small until the last one.

\paragraph{Subproblem.} Maximising $\varphi$ over weights supported on $F$ with no
sign restriction is, by~\eqref{eq:kkt} without the inequalities, the requirement
that $x$ be equidistant from $\{q_j\}_{j\in F}$ and lie in their affine hull.
With $D$ the $(m-1)\times d$ matrix of edges $e_j=q_j-q_0$ and $x=q_0+D\T y$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:sub}
  (DD\T)\,y=\tfrac12\bigl(\norm{e_1}^2,\dots,\norm{e_{m-1}}^2\bigr)\T,
  \qquad w=\Bigl(1-\textstyle\sum_j y_j,\ y\Bigr),
\end{equation}
a dense system of order $m-1\le d$, non-singular exactly when $F$ is affinely
independent. This is the circumcentre of the face, expressed in barycentric
coordinates.

\paragraph{Dropping.} If $\min_j w_j<0$ the circumcentre lies outside
$\conv\{q_j\}$ and the subproblem solution is dual-infeasible. Move along
$s=w-u$ by the ratio test
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ratio}
  \alpha=\min\{-u_i/s_i:\ s_i<0\},
\end{equation}
which drives at least one weight to zero; remove it from $F$.

\paragraph{Degeneracy.} If $F$ is affinely dependent, $DD\T$ is singular. The
directions $s$ with $\mathbf{1}\T s=0$ and $\sum_i s_iq_i=0$ --- equivalently
$D\T s_{1:}=0$ after eliminating $s_0$ --- leave $x(u)$ fixed, so
$\norm{x(u)}^2$ is frozen and $\varphi(u+ts)=\varphi(u)+t\sum_is_i\norm{p_i}^2$
is linear: the subproblem is unbounded. Project the gradient onto that null space
and apply~\eqref{eq:ratio}, shrinking $F$ by one.

\paragraph{Adding.} Otherwise accept $u\leftarrow w$, set $x=x(u)$ and
$R=\max_{i\in F}\norm{p_i-x}$, and let $k=\argmax_i\norm{p_i-x}$. If
$\norm{p_k-x}\le R$ then~\eqref{eq:kkt} holds and $(R,x,u)$ is optimal; otherwise
append $k$ to $F$ with weight zero. By~\eqref{eq:gradient-note} below this is
also the steepest-ascent choice among the fixed variables.

\begin{center}
\fbox{\begin{minipage}{0.95\textwidth}
\small
\textbf{Input} $p_1,\dots,p_n$. Recentre on $\bar p$. Set $F=\{k\}$, $k$ farthest
from $p_1$; $u_k=1$.\\
\textbf{Repeat:} if $F$ affinely dependent, take the null-space descent step and
drop; else solve~\eqref{eq:sub}. If $\min w<0$, take the ratio step~\eqref{eq:ratio}
and drop. Else set $u=w$, $x=x(u)$, $R=\max_{i\in F}\norm{p_i-x}$; if no point
exceeds $R$, return $(R,\,x+\bar p,\,u)$; else drop zero weights and append
$\argmax_i\norm{p_i-x}$ with weight $0$.
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

Each iteration either strictly increases $\varphi$ or strictly shrinks $F$, and
there are finitely many working sets, so the method is finite; as with any
active-set method a degenerate zero-length step requires an anti-cycling rule in
exact arithmetic --- for this problem, a Bland-type order on the points, which
Fischer, G\"artner and Kutz~\cite{fischer2003} prove terminating and then reject
as too slow. In practice an iteration cap serves as a safety net. Note
that
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gradient-note}
  \partial\varphi/\partial u_k-\partial\varphi/\partial u_i
    =\norm{p_k-x}^2-\norm{p_i-x}^2,
\end{equation}
so the farthest violator is the fixed variable with the largest reduced gradient.

\section{The primal pivoting method of Fischer, G\"artner and Kutz}
\label{sec:fgk}

The method of Fischer, G\"artner and Kutz~\cite{fischer2003} carries a ball rather
than a set of weights, and never surrenders it. Its state is a pair $(c,T)$, a
centre and a support set, subject to the invariant of their Fig.~2:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:fgk-invariant}
  S\subseteq B(c,T),\qquad T\subseteq\partial B(c,T),\qquad
  T\ \text{affinely independent},
\end{equation}
where $B(c,T)$ is the ball about $c$ through the farthest point of $T$. By
Seidel's lemma the pair is optimal as soon as $c\in\conv T$, and $c$ is then the
circumcentre $\mathrm{cc}(T)$ without further argument --- it lies in $\aff(T)$
and~\eqref{eq:fgk-invariant} has it equidistant from $T$. So the loop runs until
$c$ reaches $\conv T$. Each iteration is a
dropping phase, entered only once $c$ has landed in $\aff(T)$, followed by a
walking phase.

\paragraph{Dropping.} $c\in\aff(T)\setminus\conv T$ means some affine coefficient
of $c$ with respect to $T$ is negative; drop such a point. The coefficients are
the same object as the barycentric weights of~\eqref{eq:sub} and come from the
same factorisation (Section~\ref{sec:qr}), but here they are read rather than
moved: there is no ratio test, because $c$ does not travel during a drop.
Their Lemmata~4 and~5 together say the dropped point cannot stop
the walk that immediately follows, which is what keeps the two phases from undoing
each other.

\paragraph{Walking.} Move $c$ along $e=\mathrm{cc}(T)-c$. Their Lemma~3
says that segment is orthogonal to $\aff(T)$, that $T$ stays
on the boundary the whole way, and that the radius strictly decreases --- the
deflation. Two consequences make the step cheap. Orthogonality makes
$\mathrm{cc}(T)$ the projection of $c$ onto $\aff(T)$, so no circumsphere is ever
solved for and one projection does the work of~\eqref{eq:sub}. And the stopping
fraction has a closed form: with $c(a)=c+ae$, a point $p$ joins the boundary when
$\norm{p-c(a)}$ equals the common support distance; the $a^2$ terms cancel, and
$\langle q-c,e\rangle=\norm{e}^2$ for every $q\in T$ leaves
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:fgk-step}
  a_p=\frac{R_c^2-\norm{p-c}^2}
           {2\bigl(\norm{e}^2-\langle p-c,\,e\rangle\bigr)},
  \qquad R_c=\max_{q\in T}\norm{q-c},
  \qquad a=\min\{1,\ \min_p a_p\}.
\end{equation}
Criterion~(1) of Lemma~4 --- that $p$ lies behind $\aff(T)$ and so cannot stop the
walk --- is exactly the statement that this denominator is non-positive, so one
sign test separates the candidates and the numerator is non-negative on the rest
by~\eqref{eq:fgk-invariant}. If $a=1$ the centre arrives at $\mathrm{cc}(T)$, lands
in $\aff(T)$, and the next iteration drops; otherwise the minimising point enters
$T$ and the ball has shrunk.

\paragraph{Pivot rules.} Ties are resolved two ways. \emph{Bland's rule}, adapted:
fix an order on $S$, drop the negative-coefficient point of smallest rank, and
admit the smallest-rank point among those that stop the walk together. Their Theorem~1
proves termination with it, degeneracies included. The
\emph{heuristic}, which is what their own code runs and what the tables below
report, drops the \emph{most} negative coefficient and admits the tied point
farthest from $\aff(T)$ --- the enlargement that leaves the support best
conditioned. The paper records Bland's rule as correct but slow.

\begin{center}
\fbox{\begin{minipage}{0.95\textwidth}
\small
\textbf{Input} $p_1,\dots,p_n$. Recentre on $\bar p$. Set $c=p_1$ and
$T=\{k\}$, $k$ farthest from $c$.\\
\textbf{Repeat:} if $c\in\aff(T)$, take the affine coefficients of $c$; if none is
negative, return $(\max_{q\in T}\norm{q-c},\,c+\bar p,\,T)$; else drop one
negative-coefficient point by the pivot rule. Otherwise walk: form
$e=\mathrm{cc}(T)-c$ as the projection of $c$ onto $\aff(T)$, take $a$
from~\eqref{eq:fgk-step} over the points with positive denominator, set
$c\leftarrow c+ae$, and if $a<1$ admit the minimising point into $T$.
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

The radius falls strictly on every walk and no configuration recurs, so the method
is finite; under Bland's rule that is Theorem~1, and under the heuristic an
iteration cap serves as the safety net, exactly as in Section~\ref{sec:method}.

\paragraph{Degeneracy, and the price of the invariant.} The
invariant~\eqref{eq:fgk-invariant} requires $T$ affinely independent, and there is
no fallback if it is not: with $\aff(T)$ of the wrong dimension the projection that
defines the walk is meaningless. So the entering point must be certified off
$\aff(T)$ before it is admitted, which is the paper's stability threshold and the
one tolerance in the method carrying units of length (Section~\ref{sec:invariance}).
Section~\ref{sec:method} makes the opposite choice: it \emph{permits} a dependent
support and answers it with a descent direction in the null space, which costs it
a rank test on every iteration and a factorisation that must survive a state an
economic $QR$ cannot represent. Neither choice dominates --- one avoids the case,
the other resolves it.

\paragraph{How the two differ.} The support sets agree, the drop criteria agree ---
a negative barycentric coordinate, in both --- and the mechanics agree nowhere
else. Section~\ref{sec:method} takes the whole step to $\mathrm{cc}(F)$ and only
then consults the data, admitting the farthest violator; this method never takes a
full step while any point is still outside, truncating the walk at whichever point
first touches the shrinking sphere, which is also the point that enters. A drop
here is immediate and followed by a resumed walk; there it is a ratio test in
weight space, moving every weight at once. And the certificate differs in kind:
Section~\ref{sec:method} finishes with weights that verify~\eqref{eq:kkt} in one
pass, while this method finishes with what it has held all along, a ball that
contains $S$. One of the paper's observations deserves flagging in that light:
almost-cospherical clouds are their \emph{hardest} inputs, points entering and
leaving $T$ repeatedly, whereas cospherical inputs are where the dual method is at
its most accurate. Both can hold at once --- theirs is a claim about iteration
counts, the other about the final answer.

\section{The support's factorisation}
\label{sec:qr}

Per iteration, both methods want the same two things from their support: whether
it is affinely independent, and where the circumcentre of its face lies. Both read
those off the same object. Let $D$ be the $r\times d$ matrix of edges
$q_j-q_0$ of the current support and let $D\T=QR$ be its economic factorisation.
Three identities then take every per-iteration quantity off $d$ and onto the
$r\times r$ block:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:qr-identities}
  DD\T=R\T R,\qquad \ker D\T=\ker R,\qquad \norm{q_j-q_0}^2=\norm{R e_j}^2 .
\end{equation}
The first makes~\eqref{eq:sub} two triangular solves against a Cholesky factor
that is already to hand, with no $O(r^2d)$ product; the second makes the
affine-dependence test of Section~\ref{sec:method} a rank test on $R$ --- note it
is the \emph{right} null space, not the one the edge matrix itself would hand you;
the third supplies the right-hand side. The pivoting method reads the same $R$ for
its projection and its coefficients.

Rebuilding that factorisation costs $O(dr^2)$ and repairing it costs $O(dr)$, and
the repair is section~4 of~\cite{fischer2003}. Three updates cover every move
either method makes: a point joining the support appends a column, a point other
than $q_0$ leaving deletes one, and $q_0$ leaving has no column of its own --- every
column being measured \emph{from} it --- so $q_1$ is promoted by a deletion
followed by a rank-one update. In a compiled language these are Givens sweeps; in
an interpreted one they have to be the library's, or a single vectorised
refactorisation will beat them and the measurement will be of the interpreter
rather than of the data structure.

Two things keep this from being a pure win. The dependent supports
Section~\ref{sec:method} permits are exactly what an economic $QR$ cannot hold ---
an update refuses a column already in the span --- so those faces are met by
refactorising, which is affordable only because the stability of the surrounding
tolerances keeps them rare. And the repair is not cheaper at every size: measured
on the clouds of Section~\ref{sec:numerics} it is worth a factor of $3.5$ at
$d=8\,000$, is at parity near $d=100$, and loses below that, where the per-update
overhead exceeds the small dense solve it replaces. An implementation therefore
dispatches on $d$ rather than committing to one route. That threshold is why
Section~\ref{sec:numerics} can report $d=1.6\cdot10^4$ at all, and why the
Gaussian clouds of Table~\ref{tab:sweep} are indifferent to it.

\section{Invariance and floating point}
\label{sec:invariance}

Two properties are load-bearing for either method and easily lost by both. Both
are consequences of refusing any tolerance tied to coordinates of magnitude
one.

\emph{Origin invariance.} Recentre on $\bar p$ before iterating, and form
$\norm{p-x}^2$ as $\sum_k(p_k-x_k)^2$ rather than
$\norm{p}^2-2p\T x+\norm{x}^2$. The expanded form cancels quantities of size
$\norm{x}^2$ to produce an answer of size $R^2$, losing every digit by which
$\norm{x}$ exceeds $R$; for a cloud of unit extent at distance $10^6$ the
rounding floor of a squared distance is then of the order of the radius itself.

\emph{Scale invariance.} Test affine dependence on the rank of $D$, not on
$[\mathbf{1};Q\T]$: the row of ones has unit entries, dominates the singular
values, and would declare any cloud of extent below $\varepsilon$ degenerate.
Size the feasibility slack of the stopping test as a relative tolerance plus an
absolute floor proportional to $\varepsilon\max_i\norm{p_i}^2$, and normalise the
null-space direction before comparing anything derived from it --- the gradient
carries units of length$^2$, the weight tolerance does not.

\emph{Which tolerance carries units.} The two methods have one apiece, and they
are not the same one. Section~\ref{sec:method} must decide when a weight has
reached zero, which is dimensionless, and when a null-space direction is
binding, which is not --- hence the normalisation above.
Section~\ref{sec:fgk} must decide when a candidate is far enough off $\aff(T)$ to
be admitted, which carries units of length and is sized off the cloud's extent;
its other tolerance, on the affine coefficients, needs no such care, being
dimensionless and bounded by $1/2$ in the optimal configuration (Lemma~2
of~\cite{fischer2003}). The pattern is the same in both: the dimensionless test
gets a constant, the dimensioned one gets the extent.

\emph{Exponent range.} Tolerances are not the whole of it. A double reaches to
$10^{308}$, so a \emph{squared} double reaches only to $10^{154}$, and both methods
square everything: the Gram matrix, the distances, the noise floor. A cloud of
extent $10^{-160}$ --- ordinary coordinates --- therefore has a Gram matrix in the
subnormals, and the solve~\eqref{eq:sub} overflows; at $10^{+160}$ the squares
saturate and a method reports $R=0$ without raising. Rescaling the centred cloud
to unit extent removes both, and the factor must be a power of two: any other
rounds, and the exactness Table~\ref{tab:bench} reports is lost with it. The
measurements below rescale in the dual method and not in the pivoting one, which
is a property of the two implementations rather than of the two methods --- the
walk of~\eqref{eq:fgk-step} would survive the same treatment unchanged.

\section{Cost, and what each method certifies}
\label{sec:cost}

Per iteration each method performs one $O(nd)$ sweep of the cloud --- squared
distances to the current centre in Section~\ref{sec:method}, the stopping
fractions~\eqref{eq:fgk-step} in Section~\ref{sec:fgk} --- and one solve or
projection of order at most $d$, repaired rather than rebuilt above the dimension
Section~\ref{sec:qr} names. Nothing of size $n$ is ever factorised by either. This
is the structural difference from an interior-point method applied
to~\eqref{eq:primal}, which factors an $n(d+1)$-row system at every step. Both
also identify the support combinatorially and solve that face exactly, rather than
approaching it from the interior and leaving the caller to decide which slacks of
size $10^{-9}$ were meant to be zero.

What they can hand back differs, and it is the practical reason to keep both.
Section~\ref{sec:fgk} is primal feasible at every step: interrupt it at any
iteration and what is on hand is an enclosing ball, correct if not yet smallest,
with a radius that only falls from here. That is the guarantee to want under a
time budget, and Section~\ref{sec:method} cannot offer it --- by
Proposition~\ref{prop:duality} its radius is a lower bound throughout, so its ball
fails to contain the cloud until the final iteration. In exchange it finishes with
the dual weights.

The weights are the conic dual of~\eqref{eq:primal} after one rescaling: the
block for point $i$ is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:conic-dual}
  z_i=u_i\Bigl(1,\ -\frac{p_i-x}{R}\Bigr)\in\mathcal{Q}^{d+1},
\end{equation}
whose tail has norm $u_i\norm{p_i-x}/R$, equal to its head exactly on the
support, and which pairs with the slack $s_i=(R,\,p_i-x)$ as
$s_i\T z_i=(u_i/R)(R^2-\norm{p_i-x}^2)=0$ --- complementarity, each factor
vanishing where the other does not. So a solver built on this method returns a
standard-form primal--dual pair, and a caller can verify optimality
from~\eqref{eq:kkt} at the cost of one pass over the data, without re-solving.

\begin{remark}[Substitutability]
  This is what makes the method usable behind a conic solver's interface rather
  than only as a special-purpose routine. The accompanying implementation
  dispatches on the cone list: zero and non-negative blocks go to a dense dual
  active-set QP method~\cite{goldfarb1983}, and equally sized second-order blocks
  matching the shape of~\eqref{eq:primal} go to Section~\ref{sec:method}, whose
  weights are what the interface asks for; Section~\ref{sec:fgk} answers the same
  program but would have to solve~\eqref{eq:sub} once at the end to produce them. A program
  carrying the right cones but a different matrix is refused rather than
  answered, which requires reconstructing the point cloud from $(A,b)$ and
  rebuilding the program for comparison. An indefinite objective is likewise
  refused rather than regularised into definiteness, since $P+\epsilon I$ answers
  a different question.
\end{remark}

\section{A numerical comparison}
\label{sec:numerics}

Table~\ref{tab:bench} runs the two methods of Sections~\ref{sec:method}
and~\ref{sec:fgk} beside two references: Clarabel~0.11.1 applied
to~\eqref{eq:primal}, and Welzl's algorithm~\cite{welzl1991}. All four compute the
same object on the same Gaussian clouds. Two quantities are reported, because
either alone would mislead.

The first is the relative enclosure error $\max_i\norm{p_i-x}/R-1$, where a
positive value means the returned ball does not in fact contain the cloud. It is
a deterministic property of the returned pair, free of timing noise. Here the
three combinatorial methods are indistinguishable --- all come in at zero or a
single ulp, against Clarabel's interior-point residuals three to five orders
larger --- because each returns a radius that is by construction the maximum
distance over its own support. The exactness is thus a property of terminating at
a basis, and neither of the two methods here has any of it to itself.

One ulp of that is worth naming, since it is the only place the three separate,
and it is a difference between the two methods of this note. Section~\ref{sec:method}
solves afresh for the circumcentre of its final support and lands on zero;
Section~\ref{sec:fgk} reports the running sum of the walks that carried its centre
there, and so shows a single positive ulp on four of the seven rows. Both identify the same support set. The difference is in the arithmetic
that produces the centre, not in the combinatorics that select it, and at
$2.2\cdot10^{-16}$ it is of no practical consequence --- it is recorded only
because a column of exact zeros beside a column of ulps invites the wrong
inference.

The second is wall-clock time, and it must be read as a comparison of
\emph{implementations}. Both methods of this note spend their time in one
vectorised sweep per iteration, whereas Welzl's recursion
evaluates a containment predicate per step in scalar Python; a good deal of the
gap in the $t$ columns is CPython against NumPy rather than one algorithm against
another. A step count is therefore given alongside each combinatorial method that
has an informative one: the circumspheres Welzl computes, and the pivot steps the
walking method takes. Both are properties of the algorithm and the input in any
language --- but they count different things, a solve over at most $d+1$ points
against a projection plus a sweep over all $n$, and so track each method against
itself rather than against the other.

Table~\ref{tab:sweep} isolates the dependence on $d$ at fixed $n$. Welzl's median
basis count rises from $191$ to $275\,574$ between $d=2$ and $d=11$, a factor
between $1.5$ and $2.7$ per dimension across the upper half of that range, while
both simplex-like methods stay under a millisecond throughout: their work per
iteration is a solve or a projection of order at most $d$, and $d$ is small.

The two step counts are what the sweep is really for, and they part company
completely. Welzl's grows by three orders of magnitude over these ten dimensions;
the pivot count grows from $5$ to $21$, by a factor of four. That is the
separation the pivoting method was built for, and it is visible here at $d\le11$
even though the regime it was built for begins far higher. The worst-seed lines
matter as much as the medians. At $d=11$ one of fifteen clouds cost $1.6$~million
basis computations, six times the median, where the worst pivot count is $52$
against a median of $21$ --- a factor of $2.5$. Both distributions have a tail
that a single measurement will not find, which is the practical form the
expected-time analysis takes; the tails are simply not the same size.

Table~\ref{tab:highd} turns both of those tables around: $n=10^3$ fixed and $d$
from $10^3$ to $1.6\cdot10^4$, which is the regime~\cite{fischer2003} was written
for and the first one in which the linear algebra rather than the combinatorics
decides the cost. Neither of the other two references appears in it. Welzl's
median basis count is already $2.8\cdot10^5$ at $d=11$, and Clarabel would be
handed a program of $n(d+1)\approx1.6\cdot10^7$ rows to factor. Both methods that
remain repair the factorisation of their support rather than rebuilding it, as
Section~\ref{sec:qr} describes, which is what lets either of them appear in this
table at all.

The step counts are comparable in this table in a way the two of
Table~\ref{tab:sweep} are not: an active-set iteration and a pivot step each cost
one solve or projection of order $r$ plus one $O(nd)$ sweep. They separate by
about a factor of two, in the direction the mechanics predict. The active-set
count equals the final support size on four of the five rows --- on Gaussian data
the method adds points and essentially never drops one, so it spends one iteration
per support point, the $d=2\,000$ row costing four extra iterations for four drops
--- while the pivoting method takes $1.2$ to $2.1$ times as many steps. That is
the price of never taking a full step: each walk is truncated by the first point of
$S$ to touch the shrinking sphere, so a point enters after a partial move rather
than after a jump to $\mathrm{cc}(F)$. The wall clocks do not separate by anything
like that factor. Their ratio sits between $1.1$ and $1.6$ with no trend in $d$,
and repeat runs of one row move it by a few percent, so it should be read as a
small constant factor rather than as a curve: with $r$ in the hundreds both methods
spend most of each iteration in the same two places, the sweep over the cloud and
the repair of the factorisation, and the pivoting method's extra steps are its
cheapest ones.

Two further things in the table are worth naming. The support size grows like
$\sqrt d$ on these clouds --- $92$ points at $d=10^3$ and $283$ at
$d=1.6\cdot10^4$, a factor near $\sqrt2$ per doubling --- so it stays an order of
magnitude or more below the Carath\'eodory bound $d+1$, and the per-iteration solve
is of that order rather than of $d$. With $O(\sqrt d)$ iterations of $O(nd)$ each
the total should scale as roughly $d^{3/2}$, and it does: a $53$-fold increase in
time over a $16$-fold increase in $d$. And the enclosure errors stay at a few ulp,
for both methods, across four doublings of $d$ --- so the exactness of
Table~\ref{tab:bench} is a property of terminating at a basis, not of small $d$.

Read against the paper being compared to, Table~\ref{tab:highd} runs to
$d=1.6\cdot10^4$, past the $2\,000$~\cite{fischer2003} reports, with the same
support on every row. Table~\ref{tab:bench} is the opposite regime and the one
their analysis says least about: their per-pivot cost does not fall with $n$ while
the $O(nd)$ sweep vectorises, and there too the two are separated by a small
constant factor rather than by anything structural, their pivot counts running
from $3$ to $90$.

None of the three comparisons is fair to its subject, and the third is the least
fair. Clarabel is built for large sparse programs of arbitrary shape, and Welzl's
recursion is being run in the least favourable language for one. The pivoting
method carries a narrower handicap, and only in Tables~\ref{tab:bench}
and~\ref{tab:sweep}: its columns there were measured with the $QR$ of the edge
matrix rebuilt at every pivot, where~\cite{fischer2003} updates it by Givens
rotations in $O(d^2)$. At $d\le20$ that costs it little, the factorisation being of
order at most $21$, which is why those two tables were left as measured and
Table~\ref{tab:highd} --- where the data structure is the whole point --- runs both
methods with it in place. At $d=20$ the pivoting method takes $2.5$ times the
active-set time; nothing in that ratio should be carried to the $d\sim10^3$ regime
their paper reports, and Table~\ref{tab:highd} is what happens there instead. That
the specialised method wins on its own problem, in its own regime, against
implementations chosen for clarity is the weakest possible claim, and the only one
made here.

\section{Relations to neighbouring methods}
\label{sec:relations}

\subsection*{Welzl's algorithm}
\label{sec:welzl}

Welzl's randomised incremental method~\cite{welzl1991} computes the same object
and, as Table~\ref{tab:bench} confirms, terminates at an exact basis just as the
two methods above do. It differs from both in how that basis is searched.

Welzl's search is combinatorial: it recurses over subsets, drawing a point $p$ at
random, solving without it, and re-solving with $p$ forced onto the boundary when
$p$ falls outside the ball just computed. The permutation drives the recursion,
the only arithmetic is the primitive that circumscribes at most $d+1$ points, and
the analysis is that of LP-type problems --- expected $O(n)$ time for fixed $d$,
with a constant known to grow superexponentially in $d$. The method of Section~\ref{sec:method}
searches instead by ascent on $\varphi$: the weights choose the pivot, a negative
barycentric coordinate calling for a drop and the farthest violator for an
addition. The search is monotone, local, deterministic, and admits add
\emph{and} drop steps; what it does not admit is an expected-time bound, and
Section~\ref{sec:method} claims none.

Table~\ref{tab:sweep} is the practical shape of that difference: at fixed $n$ the
recursion's cost climbs by a factor of two or so per dimension and carries a long
upper tail, while a solve of order $d$ does not notice $d$ in this range. The
other difference is the certificate. Welzl's recursion yields the ball and its
basis; the method above yields the barycentric weights as well, which are what
make~\eqref{eq:kkt} checkable in one pass and what become~\eqref{eq:conic-dual}
when a conic interface asks for a dual. The weights can be recovered from a basis
by solving~\eqref{eq:sub}, but they are not a by-product of the search there,
whereas here they \emph{are} the search --- which is also why a support set warm
starts a perturbed cloud and a fresh random permutation cannot.

G\"artner's robust implementation~\cite{gaertner1999} addresses the fragility of
the recursion's predicates; Section~\ref{sec:invariance} answers the same question here.


\subsection*{The minimum-norm point problem}
\label{sec:minnorm}

If all points have equal norm $\rho$ --- after the standard lift of a kernel
problem onto a sphere, say --- then~\eqref{eq:phi} reduces to
$\varphi(u)=\rho^2-\norm{x(u)}^2$, and maximising it over $\Delta_n$ is
minimising $\norm{x}$ over $\conv S$: the minimum-norm point problem of
Wolfe~\cite{wolfe1976} and of the Frank--Wolfe family~\cite{frank1956}. The
working set is then Wolfe's corral, the subproblem~\eqref{eq:sub} his affine
minimiser, the drop step his major cycle. Restoring the linear term
$\sum_iu_i\norm{p_i}^2$ gives the general case, and is what makes the subproblem
a circumcentre rather than a projection.

\section{Notes}
\label{sec:notes}

Of the two exact lines of work Section~\ref{sec:intro} sketches, the method of
Section~\ref{sec:fgk} is the geometric one and that of Section~\ref{sec:method}
the quadratic-programming one, after G\"artner and
Sch\"onherr~\cite{gaertner2000}, whose reservation that a QP formulation needed
exact arithmetic past a few hundred dimensions Table~\ref{tab:highd} bears on
without settling: in double precision the method returns a ball out to
$d=1.6\cdot10^4$ whose enclosure error is a few ulp, on Gaussian clouds, which are
not the inputs that reservation was about. The QP machinery is standard~\cite{nocedal2006}. An
implementation of the method, of the pivoting method of Section~\ref{sec:fgk}, of the
Goldfarb--Idnani core, and of the conic front end is available at
\url{https://github.com/tschm/cvxball}, where the two methods this note compares
ship side by side and the Clarabel and Welzl routes of Table~\ref{tab:bench} sit
beside them as references.
