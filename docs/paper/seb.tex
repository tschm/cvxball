\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Two active-set methods solve the smallest enclosing ball problem from opposite
sides: a dual ascent over the unit simplex, which holds no enclosing ball until
it terminates, and the primal deflation of Fischer, G\"artner and
Kutz~\cite{fischer2003}, which is feasible at every step. They keep the same
support set, solve the same subproblem, and can repair the same $QR$
factorisation of the same edge matrix in $O(dr)$. We give the conditions under
which either survives floating point, and then hold that linear algebra fixed to
measure what actually separates them.

The conditions are sharper than tolerance hygiene. Each method carries exactly
one tolerance with units of length and one with none, and the two must be sized
differently; the affine-dependence test must run on the edge matrix rather than
on the system bordered by a row of ones, which would call any cloud of small
extent degenerate; and since a \emph{squared} double reaches only $10^{154}$,
the centred cloud must be rescaled by an exact power of two --- any other factor
rounds, and the exactness goes with it.

With those in place both methods return a ball whose enclosure error is a few
ulp on Gaussian clouds to $d=1.6\cdot10^4$, eight times the largest dimension
previously reported for the pivoting method, agreeing on the support set on
every row; the support grows as $\sqrt d$ rather than as $d$, and time with it
as $d^{3/2}$. What remains between the two is a factor of $1.2$ to $2.1$ in
steps --- the price of the primal method never taking a full step --- which the
wall clock damps to between $1.1$ and $1.6$, and a difference in what each can
hand back: dual weights that certify optimality in one pass, against a ball that
is already valid if the iteration is cut short.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Sylvester stated the problem in 1857 in a single sentence~\cite{sylvester1857}:
given a finite set of points in the plane, find the smallest circle containing
all of them. It has been a standard example ever since --- short enough to state
in a line, geometric enough to draw, and just hard enough that the obvious
algorithms are wrong. It is the $1$-centre facility location problem, where one
facility is placed so that the farthest customer travels as little as possible.
It is the primitive under bounding-sphere hierarchies in graphics and collision
detection. As the minimum enclosing ball of a set of feature vectors it is the
core-set problem behind kernel machines. And it is a fixture of solver
documentation: Hexaly ships it as a modelling template~\cite{hexaly}, CGAL as
\texttt{Min\_sphere\_d} --- Welzl's recursion for small inputs, G\"artner's
method for large ones.

The algorithmic history is largely a computational-geometry one. Welzl's
randomised incremental method~\cite{welzl1991} solves it in expected $O(n)$ time
for fixed $d$ by recursing over subsets with a few points forced onto the
boundary, with a constant that grows superexponentially in $d$; much of what
followed is a response to that. G\"artner~\cite{gaertner1999} made the recursion's
predicates robust in floating point, G\"artner and Sch\"onherr~\cite{gaertner2000}
recast the problem as a quadratic program and solved it exactly, reserving
judgment past a few hundred dimensions, and Fischer, G\"artner and
Kutz~\cite{fischer2003} left the recursion behind for a pivoting method that
deflates an enclosing ball, carrying a dynamic $QR$ of the support's edge matrix
and reporting $d$ in the thousands --- a finite algorithm of that second kind
going back to Elzinga and Hearn~\cite{elzinga1972}.

What returns the problem to an optimizer's desk is that its dual is a concave
quadratic over the unit simplex: the active set is the set of points on the
boundary sphere, and the certificate is geometric rather than algebraic --- the
points carrying weight are exactly the points at distance $R$, which a caller can
check in one pass. Active-set machinery is less comfortable on feasible sets with
more structure~\cite{schmelzer2026gi,schmelzer2026nncg}; here there is none to
speak of.

Two things follow. The first is a set of conditions under which either method
survives floating point, in Section~\ref{sec:invariance}: which of each method's
two tolerances carries units of length and which carries none, why the
affine-dependence test must run on the edge matrix rather than on the system
bordered by a row of ones, and why the rescaling that keeps a squared double
inside the exponent range has to be by an exact power of two. They are stated for
both methods because both need them, and they are what lets the measurements
below reach the dimensions they do.

The second is a comparison with the linear algebra held fixed. The dual ascent of
Section~\ref{sec:method} and the primal deflation of Section~\ref{sec:fgk} ---
the pivoting method of Fischer, G\"artner and Kutz~\cite{fischer2003} --- keep the
same support set, solve the same subproblem and repair the same factorisation
(Section~\ref{sec:qr}). Handing both the same data structure removes the usual
confound, and what is left is a property of the two searches rather than of two
implementations: on every input measured in Section~\ref{sec:numerics} they agree
on the ball and on its support, and separate by about a factor of two in steps and
by less in time. Where they part is in what they hold while they run, and so in
what they can promise if interrupted.

\section{The problem and its two sides}
\label{sec:dual}

For $S=\{p_1,\dots,p_n\}\subset\R^d$ the smallest enclosing ball is the solution of
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:primal}
  \min_{x,r}\ r \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \norm{p_i-x}\le r,\ i=1,\dots,n,
\end{equation}
a second-order cone program in $(r,x)\in\R^{1+d}$ with $n$ blocks
$(r,\,p_i-x)\in\mathcal{Q}^{d+1}$. Existence follows from coercivity of
$f(x)=\max_i\norm{p_i-x}$ and uniqueness from strict convexity of $f^2$ along any
segment; we write $(R,x^\star)$ for the optimum.

Let $\Delta_n=\{u\in\R^n: u\ge0,\ \mathbf{1}\T u=1\}$, and for $u\in\Delta_n$ put
$x(u)=\sum_i u_ip_i$ and
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:phi}
  \varphi(u)=\sum_i u_i\norm{p_i}^2-\norm{x(u)}^2 ,
\end{equation}
a concave quadratic. Everything below rests on one identity, valid for all
$y\in\R^d$ and $u\in\Delta_n$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:id}
  \sum_i u_i\norm{p_i-y}^2
    \;=\; \sum_i u_i\norm{p_i}^2-2\,x(u)\T y+\norm{y}^2
    \;=\; \varphi(u)+\norm{x(u)-y}^2 .
\end{equation}
Both steps are mechanical: expand and collect, using $\sum_iu_ip_i=x(u)$ on the
cross term and $\mathbf{1}\T u=1$ on $\norm{y}^2$, then add and subtract
$\norm{x(u)}^2$. It is the parallel-axis identity --- measuring from anywhere
other than the weighted mean costs precisely $\norm{x(u)-y}^2$, whatever the
weights, and $\varphi(u)$ is what is left when $y$ is the mean.

\begin{proposition}
  \label{prop:duality}
  $R^2=\max_{u\in\Delta_n}\varphi(u)$, and $x^\star=x(w)$ for any maximiser $w$.
\end{proposition}

\noindent
Both directions are short: $\varphi\le R^2$ is~\eqref{eq:id} applied to any
enclosing ball, and equality follows by separating $x^\star$ from $\conv T$,
$T=\{p_i:\norm{p_i-x^\star}=R\}$, if the two were disjoint. See
also~\cite{gaertner2000}.

Since $\partial\varphi/\partial u_i=\norm{p_i-x(u)}^2-\norm{x(u)}^2$, the
first-order conditions for $\max_{\Delta_n}\varphi$ read, after cancelling the
common term,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:kkt}
  \norm{p_i-x}=R\ \ (w_i>0),\qquad \norm{p_i-x}\le R\ \ (w_i=0),
\end{equation}
the geometric certificate: the points carrying weight lie on the boundary sphere
and the rest are enclosed. By Carath\'eodory the support may be taken of size at
most $d+1$, so~\eqref{eq:primal} is determined by at most $d+1$ of the $n$ points
and the problem is combinatorial in the choice of that subset.

Read forwards,~\eqref{eq:kkt} is a search for weights: keep a set of points on the
sphere, adjust the weights, stop when no point lies outside. Read backwards it is
a search for balls: keep a ball containing $S$ with a set $T$ on its boundary, and
shrink it until its centre falls inside $\conv T$. The second rests on a fact due
to Seidel, recorded in~\cite{gaertner2000} and Lemma~1 of~\cite{fischer2003}: if
$T$ lies on the boundary of a ball $B$ centred at $c$, then $B$ is the smallest
ball enclosing $T$ if and only if $c\in\conv T$.
With $S\subseteq B$ and $T\subseteq S$ the inclusions
$\mathrm{seb}(T)\subseteq\mathrm{seb}(S)\subseteq B$ close, so a ball containing
the cloud, carrying $T$ on its boundary and centred inside $\conv T$ is \emph{the}
answer --- and $\conv T$ is the only thing left to check.
Sections~\ref{sec:method} and~\ref{sec:fgk} are those two readings made into
methods. They keep the same kind of state --- a subset of $S$ that will end up on
the boundary, and a point in its affine hull --- and differ in which feasibility
they refuse to give up. The first stays dual feasible, so by
Proposition~\ref{prop:duality} its radius rises to $R$ from below and its ball
encloses nothing until the last iteration; the second stays primal feasible, so
its radius falls to $R$ from above and every iterate is an answer, merely not yet
the best one.

\section{The dual active-set method}
\label{sec:method}

Maintain a working set $F$ and weights $u>0$ on it supported there and summing to
one:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:as-invariant}
  u\in\Delta_n,\qquad \mathrm{supp}(u)\subseteq F,\qquad
  \varphi(u)\ \text{non-decreasing},
\end{equation}
the dual counterpart of the invariant~\eqref{eq:fgk-invariant} carried by the
method of Section~\ref{sec:fgk}. This is a primal active-set method run on the
dual~\eqref{eq:phi}: the free variables are the weights of the support, every
subproblem solves one face exactly, and feasibility for the original problem
arrives only at the end --- by Proposition~\ref{prop:duality},
$\sqrt{\varphi(u)}$ is a lower bound on $R$ at every iterate, so the ball on hand
is too small until the last one.

\paragraph{Subproblem.} Maximising $\varphi$ over weights supported on $F$ with no
sign restriction is, by~\eqref{eq:kkt} without the inequalities, the requirement
that $x$ be equidistant from $\{q_j\}_{j\in F}$ and lie in their affine hull.
With $D$ the $(m-1)\times d$ matrix of edges $e_j=q_j-q_0$ and $x=q_0+D\T y$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:sub}
  (DD\T)\,y=\tfrac12\bigl(\norm{e_1}^2,\dots,\norm{e_{m-1}}^2\bigr)\T,
  \qquad w=\Bigl(1-\textstyle\sum_j y_j,\ y\Bigr),
\end{equation}
a dense system of order $m-1\le d$, non-singular exactly when $F$ is affinely
independent. This is the circumcentre of the face, expressed in barycentric
coordinates.

\paragraph{Dropping.} If $\min_j w_j<0$ the circumcentre lies outside
$\conv\{q_j\}$ and the subproblem solution is dual-infeasible. Move along
$s=w-u$ by the ratio test
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ratio}
  \alpha=\min\{-u_i/s_i:\ s_i<0\},
\end{equation}
which drives at least one weight to zero; remove it from $F$.

\paragraph{Degeneracy.} If $F$ is affinely dependent, $DD\T$ is singular. The
directions $s$ with $\mathbf{1}\T s=0$ and $\sum_i s_iq_i=0$ --- equivalently
$D\T s_{1:}=0$ after eliminating $s_0$ --- leave $x(u)$ fixed, so
$\norm{x(u)}^2$ is frozen and $\varphi(u+ts)=\varphi(u)+t\sum_is_i\norm{p_i}^2$
is linear: the subproblem is unbounded. Project the gradient onto that null space
and apply~\eqref{eq:ratio}, shrinking $F$ by one.

\paragraph{Adding.} Otherwise accept $u\leftarrow w$, set $x=x(u)$ and
$R=\max_{i\in F}\norm{p_i-x}$, and let $k=\argmax_i\norm{p_i-x}$. If
$\norm{p_k-x}\le R$ then~\eqref{eq:kkt} holds and $(R,x,u)$ is optimal; otherwise
append $k$ to $F$ with weight zero. By~\eqref{eq:gradient-note} below this is
also the steepest-ascent choice among the fixed variables.

Each iteration either strictly increases $\varphi$ or strictly shrinks $F$, and
there are finitely many working sets, so the method is finite; a degenerate
zero-length step needs an anti-cycling rule in exact arithmetic, and in practice
an iteration cap serves as the safety net. Note that
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gradient-note}
  \partial\varphi/\partial u_k-\partial\varphi/\partial u_i
    =\norm{p_k-x}^2-\norm{p_i-x}^2,
\end{equation}
so the farthest violator is the fixed variable with the largest reduced gradient.

\section{The primal pivoting method of Fischer, G\"artner and Kutz}
\label{sec:fgk}

The method of Fischer, G\"artner and Kutz~\cite{fischer2003} carries a ball rather
than a set of weights, and never surrenders it. Its state is a pair $(c,T)$, a
centre and a support set, subject to the invariant of their Fig.~2:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:fgk-invariant}
  S\subseteq B(c,T),\qquad T\subseteq\partial B(c,T),\qquad
  T\ \text{affinely independent},
\end{equation}
where $B(c,T)$ is the ball about $c$ through the farthest point of $T$. By
Seidel's lemma the pair is optimal as soon as $c\in\conv T$, and $c$ is then the
circumcentre $\mathrm{cc}(T)$ without further argument. Each iteration is a
dropping phase, entered only once $c$ has landed in $\aff(T)$, followed by a
walking phase.

\paragraph{Dropping.} $c\in\aff(T)\setminus\conv T$ means some affine coefficient
of $c$ with respect to $T$ is negative; drop such a point. The coefficients are
the same object as the barycentric weights of~\eqref{eq:sub} and come from the
same factorisation (Section~\ref{sec:qr}), but here they are read rather than
moved: there is no ratio test, because $c$ does not travel during a drop.
Their Lemmata~4 and~5 together say the dropped point cannot stop
the walk that immediately follows, which is what keeps the two phases from undoing
each other.

\paragraph{Walking.} Move $c$ along $e=\mathrm{cc}(T)-c$. Their Lemma~3
says that segment is orthogonal to $\aff(T)$, that $T$ stays
on the boundary the whole way, and that the radius strictly decreases --- the
deflation. Two consequences make the step cheap. Orthogonality makes
$\mathrm{cc}(T)$ the projection of $c$ onto $\aff(T)$, so no circumsphere is ever
solved for and one projection does the work of~\eqref{eq:sub}. And the stopping
fraction has a closed form: with $c(a)=c+ae$, a point $p$ joins the boundary when
$\norm{p-c(a)}$ equals the common support distance; the $a^2$ terms cancel, and
$\langle q-c,e\rangle=\norm{e}^2$ for every $q\in T$ leaves
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:fgk-step}
  a_p=\frac{R_c^2-\norm{p-c}^2}
           {2\bigl(\norm{e}^2-\langle p-c,\,e\rangle\bigr)},
  \qquad R_c=\max_{q\in T}\norm{q-c},
  \qquad a=\min\{1,\ \min_p a_p\}.
\end{equation}
Criterion~(1) of Lemma~4 --- that $p$ lies behind $\aff(T)$ and so cannot stop the
walk --- is exactly the statement that this denominator is non-positive, so one
sign test separates the candidates and the numerator is non-negative on the rest
by~\eqref{eq:fgk-invariant}. If $a=1$ the centre arrives at $\mathrm{cc}(T)$, lands
in $\aff(T)$, and the next iteration drops; otherwise the minimising point enters
$T$ and the ball has shrunk.

\paragraph{Pivot rules.} Ties are resolved two ways. Bland's rule, adapted --- fix
an order on $S$, take the smallest-rank candidate at both the drop and the
admission --- is what their Theorem~1 proves terminating, degeneracies included,
and is also recorded there as slow. The heuristic, which their own code runs and
which the table below reports, drops the \emph{most} negative coefficient and
admits the tied point farthest from $\aff(T)$, leaving the support best
conditioned.

The radius falls strictly on every walk and no configuration recurs, so the method
is finite; under Bland's rule that is Theorem~1, and under the heuristic an
iteration cap serves as the safety net, exactly as in Section~\ref{sec:method}.

\paragraph{Degeneracy, and the price of the invariant.} The
invariant~\eqref{eq:fgk-invariant} requires $T$ affinely independent, and there is
no fallback if it is not: with $\aff(T)$ of the wrong dimension the projection that
defines the walk is meaningless. So the entering point must be certified off
$\aff(T)$ before it is admitted --- the paper's stability threshold, and the one
tolerance in the method carrying units of length (Section~\ref{sec:invariance}).
Section~\ref{sec:method} instead \emph{permits} a dependent support and answers it
with a descent direction in the null space, paying a rank test every iteration and
a factorisation that must survive a state an economic $QR$ cannot represent.
Neither choice dominates --- one avoids the case, the other resolves it.

\paragraph{How the two differ.} The support sets agree and the drop criteria agree
--- a negative barycentric coordinate, in both --- and the mechanics agree nowhere
else. Section~\ref{sec:method} takes the whole step to $\mathrm{cc}(F)$ and only
then consults the data, admitting the farthest violator; this method never takes a
full step while any point is still outside, truncating the walk at whichever point
first touches the shrinking sphere, which is also the point that enters. A drop
here is immediate and followed by a resumed walk; there it is a ratio test in
weight space, moving every weight at once. That difference is what
Section~\ref{sec:numerics} measures once the linear algebra is held fixed.

\section{The support's factorisation}
\label{sec:qr}

Per iteration, both methods want the same two things from their support: whether
it is affinely independent, and where the circumcentre of its face lies. Both read
those off the same object. Let $D$ be the $r\times d$ matrix of edges
$q_j-q_0$ of the current support and let $D\T=QR$ be its economic factorisation.
Three identities then take every per-iteration quantity off $d$ and onto the
$r\times r$ block:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:qr-identities}
  DD\T=R\T R,\qquad \ker D\T=\ker R,\qquad \norm{q_j-q_0}^2=\norm{R e_j}^2 .
\end{equation}
The first makes~\eqref{eq:sub} two triangular solves against a Cholesky factor
that is already to hand, with no $O(r^2d)$ product; the second makes the
affine-dependence test of Section~\ref{sec:method} a rank test on $R$ --- note it
is the \emph{right} null space, not the one the edge matrix itself would hand you;
the third supplies the right-hand side. The pivoting method reads the same $R$ for
its projection and its coefficients.

Rebuilding that factorisation costs $O(dr^2)$ and repairing it costs $O(dr)$, and
the repair is section~4 of~\cite{fischer2003}. Three updates cover every move
either method makes: a point joining the support appends a column, a point other
than $q_0$ leaving deletes one, and $q_0$ leaving has no column of its own --- every
column being measured \emph{from} it --- so $q_1$ is promoted by a deletion
followed by a rank-one update. In a compiled language these are Givens sweeps; in
an interpreted one they have to be the library's, or a single vectorised
refactorisation will beat them and the measurement will be of the interpreter
rather than of the data structure.

Two things keep this from being a pure win. The dependent supports
Section~\ref{sec:method} permits are exactly what an economic $QR$ cannot hold ---
an update refuses a column already in the span --- so those faces are met by
refactorising, affordable only because the surrounding tolerances keep them rare.
And the repair is not cheaper at every size: on the clouds of
Section~\ref{sec:numerics} it is worth a factor of $3.5$ at $d=8\,000$, is at
parity near $d=100$, and loses below that. An implementation therefore dispatches
on $d$ rather than committing to one route. That threshold is why
Section~\ref{sec:numerics} can report $d=1.6\cdot10^4$ at all, and why a cloud of
low dimension is indifferent to it.

\section{Invariance and floating point}
\label{sec:invariance}

Two properties are load-bearing for either method and easily lost by both. Both
are consequences of refusing any tolerance tied to coordinates of magnitude
one.

\emph{Origin invariance.} Recentre on $\bar p$ before iterating, and form
$\norm{p-x}^2$ as $\sum_k(p_k-x_k)^2$ rather than
$\norm{p}^2-2p\T x+\norm{x}^2$. The expanded form cancels quantities of size
$\norm{x}^2$ to produce an answer of size $R^2$, losing every digit by which
$\norm{x}$ exceeds $R$; for a cloud of unit extent at distance $10^6$ the
rounding floor of a squared distance is then of the order of the radius itself.

\emph{Scale invariance.} Test affine dependence on the rank of $D$, not on
$[\mathbf{1};Q\T]$: the row of ones has unit entries, dominates the singular
values, and would declare any cloud of extent below $\varepsilon$ degenerate.
Size the feasibility slack of the stopping test as a relative tolerance plus an
absolute floor proportional to $\varepsilon\max_i\norm{p_i}^2$, and normalise the
null-space direction before comparing anything derived from it --- the gradient
carries units of length$^2$, the weight tolerance does not.

\emph{Which tolerance carries units.} The two methods have one apiece, and they
are not the same one. Section~\ref{sec:method} must decide when a weight has
reached zero, which is dimensionless, and when a null-space direction is
binding, which is not --- hence the normalisation above.
Section~\ref{sec:fgk} must decide when a candidate is far enough off $\aff(T)$ to
be admitted, which carries units of length and is sized off the cloud's extent;
its other tolerance, on the affine coefficients, needs no such care, being
dimensionless and bounded by $1/2$ in the optimal configuration (Lemma~2
of~\cite{fischer2003}). The pattern is the same in both: the dimensionless test
gets a constant, the dimensioned one gets the extent.

\emph{Exponent range.} Tolerances are not the whole of it. A double reaches to
$10^{308}$, so a \emph{squared} double reaches only to $10^{154}$, and both methods
square everything: the Gram matrix, the distances, the noise floor. A cloud of
extent $10^{-160}$ --- ordinary coordinates --- therefore has a Gram matrix in the
subnormals, and the solve~\eqref{eq:sub} overflows; at $10^{+160}$ the squares
saturate and a method reports $R=0$ without raising. Rescaling the centred cloud
to unit extent removes both, and the factor must be a power of two: any other
rounds, and the exactness Table~\ref{tab:highd} reports is lost with it. The
measurements below rescale in the dual method and not in the pivoting one, which
is a property of the two implementations rather than of the two methods --- the
walk of~\eqref{eq:fgk-step} would survive the same treatment unchanged.

\section{Cost, and what each method certifies}
\label{sec:cost}

Per iteration each method performs one $O(nd)$ sweep of the cloud --- squared
distances to the centre in Section~\ref{sec:method}, the stopping
fractions~\eqref{eq:fgk-step} in Section~\ref{sec:fgk} --- and one solve or
projection of order at most $d$. Nothing of size $n$ is ever factorised, which is
the structural difference from an interior-point method applied
to~\eqref{eq:primal}, and both solve the final face exactly rather than leaving
the caller to decide which slacks of size $10^{-9}$ were meant to be zero.

What they can hand back differs, and it is the practical reason to keep both.
Section~\ref{sec:fgk} is primal feasible at every step: interrupt it at any
iteration and what is on hand is an enclosing ball, correct if not yet smallest,
with a radius that only falls from here. That is the guarantee to want under a
time budget, and Section~\ref{sec:method} cannot offer it --- by
Proposition~\ref{prop:duality} its radius is a lower bound throughout, so its ball
fails to contain the cloud until the final iteration. In exchange it finishes with
the dual weights.

Those weights are the conic dual of~\eqref{eq:primal} after one rescaling ---
$z_i=u_i(1,\,-(p_i-x)/R)$, which lies in $\mathcal{Q}^{d+1}$, is tight exactly on
the support, and pairs with the slack $s_i=(R,\,p_i-x)$ to give $s_i\T z_i=0$.
A solver built on this method therefore returns a standard-form primal--dual
pair, and a caller can verify optimality from~\eqref{eq:kkt} in one pass over the
data, without re-solving. That is what makes it usable behind a conic interface
rather than only as a special-purpose routine; Section~\ref{sec:fgk} answers the
same program but would have to solve~\eqref{eq:sub} once at the end to produce
the weights.

\section{A numerical comparison}
\label{sec:numerics}

Table~\ref{tab:highd} fixes $n=10^3$ and takes $d$ from $10^3$ to
$1.6\cdot10^4$, the regime~\cite{fischer2003} was written for and the first in
which the linear algebra rather than the combinatorics decides the cost. Two
quantities are reported, because either alone would mislead. The first is the
relative enclosure error $\max_i\norm{p_i-x}/R-1$, a deterministic property of the
returned pair, free of timing noise, where a positive value means the ball does
not in fact contain the cloud. The second is wall-clock time, which must be read
as a comparison of \emph{implementations}: both methods spend most of each
iteration in one vectorised sweep and one repair of the factorisation, so the step
counts beside them are what carry over to another language.

Neither reference appears in the table. Welzl's median basis count is already
$2.8\cdot10^5$ at $d=11$, and Clarabel would be handed a program of
$n(d+1)\approx1.6\cdot10^7$ rows to factor. Both methods that remain repair the
factorisation of their support rather than rebuilding it (Section~\ref{sec:qr}),
which is what lets either appear here at all.

An active-set iteration and a pivot step each cost one solve or projection of
order $r$ plus one $O(nd)$ sweep, so the two step counts are comparable here. They separate by
about a factor of two, in the direction the mechanics predict. The active-set count
equals the final support size on four of the five rows --- on Gaussian data the
method adds points and essentially never drops one --- while the pivoting method
takes $1.2$ to $2.1$ times as many steps, the price of never taking a full step:
each walk is truncated by the first point of $S$ to touch the shrinking sphere.
The wall clocks do not separate by anything like that factor. Their ratio sits
between $1.1$ and $1.6$ with no trend in $d$, and repeat runs move it by a few
percent, so it is a small constant rather than a curve: with $r$ in the hundreds
both methods spend most of each iteration in the same two places, and the pivoting
method's extra steps are its cheapest ones.

Two further things are worth naming. The support grows like $\sqrt d$ --- $92$
points at $d=10^3$ and $283$ at $d=1.6\cdot10^4$ --- staying an order of magnitude
below the Carath\'eodory bound $d+1$, so the per-iteration solve is of that order
rather than of $d$; with $O(\sqrt d)$ iterations of $O(nd)$ each the total should
scale as $d^{3/2}$, and it does, a $53$-fold increase in time over a $16$-fold
increase in $d$. And the enclosure errors stay at a few ulp for both methods
across four doublings of $d$: the exactness is a property of terminating at a
basis, not of small $d$.

The table runs past the $d=2\,000$ of~\cite{fischer2003} with the same support on
every row, and both columns were measured with the factorisation repaired rather
than rebuilt --- so the ratio between them measures what the two searches cost and
not what two data structures cost. Neither is a tuned implementation, and their
agreement to within a small constant factor is therefore a claim about the methods
rather than about the code.

\section{Notes}
\label{sec:notes}

Welzl's recursion searches the same basis combinatorially, driven by a random
permutation, with the analysis of LP-type problems behind it; both methods here
search by pivoting instead, monotonically and deterministically, and claim no
expected-time bound in exchange. If all points share a norm --- after the standard
lift of a kernel problem onto a sphere, say ---
then~\eqref{eq:phi} reduces to $\varphi(u)=\rho^2-\norm{x(u)}^2$ and
Section~\ref{sec:method} becomes the minimum-norm point method of
Wolfe~\cite{wolfe1976}, its working set his corral and~\eqref{eq:sub} his affine
minimiser; the linear term $\sum_iu_i\norm{p_i}^2$ is what makes the subproblem a
circumcentre rather than a projection.

Of the two exact lines of work Section~\ref{sec:intro} sketches, the method of
Section~\ref{sec:fgk} is the geometric one and that of Section~\ref{sec:method}
the quadratic-programming one, after G\"artner and
Sch\"onherr~\cite{gaertner2000}, whose reservation that a QP formulation needed
exact arithmetic past a few hundred dimensions Table~\ref{tab:highd} bears on
without settling: in double precision the method returns a ball out to
$d=1.6\cdot10^4$ whose enclosure error is a few ulp, on Gaussian clouds, which are
not the inputs that reservation was about. The QP machinery is standard~\cite{nocedal2006}. An
implementation of the method, of the pivoting method of Section~\ref{sec:fgk}, of the
Goldfarb--Idnani core, and of the conic front end is available at
\url{https://github.com/tschm/cvxball}, where the two methods this note compares
ship side by side with the Clarabel and Welzl routes beside them as
references.
